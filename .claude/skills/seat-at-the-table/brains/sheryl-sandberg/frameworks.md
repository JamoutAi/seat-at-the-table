# Frameworks: Sheryl Sandberg

25 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (choose the bet, time the money, scale the operation, lead the people, stay close to customers, recover from the hit). "Sources" lists the sources in the corpus that use the framework or a clear synonym of it. Book tags show where a framework sits in her books, as she teaches them in public; no book text is used. Quotes come from auto captions, so they keep caption quirks. Several of her best operating lessons are credited to other people (Eric Schmidt, Larry Page, Bill Campbell, Meg Whitman, Adam Grant, Dave Goldberg); the brain says so wherever it applies.

## Choose the bet

### 1. The rocket ship test
- **What it is:** Pick the opportunity by growth, impact, and mission, not by title, level, or how well it fits a checklist. When a company grows fast, everyone in it grows; when it grows slowly, you get politics. She tells the story of Eric Schmidt putting his hand on her job spreadsheet in 2001: the Google job met none of her criteria, and he told her to take it anyway (S013, S031, S056).
- **Parts:** (1) Is the thing growing fast, or does it have a credible path to fast growth? (2) Does the mission matter to you? (3) Will you have impact, regardless of title? (4) Is the unglamorous core job available? She took the four-person self-serve AdWords team, which Omid Kordestani called "a tractor job" (S053). (5) Lateral or lower is fine: both her Google and Facebook jobs were more junior than her other offers (S031).
- **When to use:** Choosing between opportunities, markets, partners, hires to chase, or a job offer. Also for a founder choosing which product line to put the best people on.
- **How to score:** Score each option 1 to 10 on growth rate and on mission fit; title and comfort score zero. She warns the phrase is now abused: companies growing 2% call themselves rocket ships, and "a rocket ship can be defined in lots of different ways" (S056). Ask for the growth evidence.
- **Quote:** "If you're offered a seat on a rocket ship, don't ask what seat. Just get on." (S013)
- **Also:** "Worry more about growth than you worry about your position." (S053)
- **Sources:** 7 (S013, S017, S031, S035, S053, S056, S008)

### 2. Prioritization: we can only do one and two
- **What it is:** The hardest job in running a company is choosing, and the hard part is not killing bad ideas but declining good ones. Facebook's early strategy was to do one thing, social technology, "incredibly well" and let partners do the rest (S011, S012).
- **Parts:** Rank the list. Fund one and two. Say no to three, even when three is real (she uses the bugs a board member kept pointing out as her example). Name the things you will not build: in 2009 she listed no editorial staff, no wallet business, no enterprise admin tools, no competing with developers' apps (S003, S004).
- **When to use:** A founder with more good ideas than people; a new product line competing with the core; any plan with five priorities.
- **How to score:** Count the priorities. More than two funded at once is a red flag. For each new idea, ask what it displaces in the top two; if the answer is "nothing," the plan is not honest about capacity.
- **Quote:** "The question of prioritization in business, in my experience, is the hardest thing you have to do when you're trying to help run a company." (S012)
- **Also:** "It's saying no to the good ones, the good ideas." (S012)
- **Sources:** 5 (S003, S004, S011, S012, S028)

### 3. Demand generation versus demand fulfillment
- **What it is:** Her monetization thesis for Facebook. Search (Google) wins the bottom of the funnel, people who already know what they want. Most marketing money, she argued, sits at the top: making people want things they did not know about. Play where the bigger pool is, and where your product is structurally advantaged (S003, S005).
- **Parts:** Map the funnel: awareness, affinity, intent, purchase. Size each stage. Ask which stage your product serves better than the incumbent. Then build the proof that it works there (framework #20).
- **When to use:** Market sizing, positioning against a dominant incumbent, deciding which customer job to own.
- **How to score:** Estimate the share of spend or need in the stage you serve. A big stage where you hold a structural edge beats a crowded stage where you are a copy.
- **Quote:** "It's 10% demand fulfillment and 90% demand generation, the top of the funnel." (S003)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S005, S006)

### 4. Self-disruption, with usage as the guardrail
- **What it is:** Most companies have one big innovation, become risk-averse protecting it, and stop evolving. The bigger risk is not changing enough. But some changes are mistakes. Her test: users protest a lot of good changes, so watch what they do, not what they say (S003, S009).
- **Parts:** Beacon versus News Feed. Both drew protests. Beacon was wrong and should have been pulled faster; News Feed drew the biggest protest ever and people used it more. Unbundling Messenger cost "a bad couple of weeks" of feedback and was worth it (S024). Pulling a right change back means you have "choken away something the world really wants" (S009).
- **When to use:** A change that customers complain about; a pivot that cannibalizes the core; a team arguing to protect the current product.
- **How to score:** After the change, does engagement or usage go up while complaints go up? Keep it. Do complaints track a real loss of trust or value? Pull it back fast. If you cannot tell, say so and set the metric that will decide.
- **Quote:** "What we are trying to avoid is the risk of being so careful to protect what we have that we stop putting out a better product for users." (S003)
- **Also:** "If it's Beacon you should pull it back fast, faster than they did." (S009) and "How do we tell which it is?" (S009)
- **Sources:** 5 (S003, S005, S009, S024, S035)

### 5. Risk for reward, then ten turns
- **What it is:** Her version of bold. "What would you do if you weren't afraid" is a Facebook poster she quotes constantly, but she says it is not permission for stupid risks: a risk needs a payoff. Then, once committed, do not stare at the whole mountain; do the next ten turns (S031, S056, S036).
- **Parts:** (1) Name the reward. (2) Name the risks, from diverse perspectives. (3) If the risks can be managed, keep going "even in the face of uncertainty" (her lesson from David Baltimore and Asilomar, S038). (4) Break execution into the next ten turns, the advice her mother gave her stuck at the top of a ski run at 16 (S036, S046, S060).
- **When to use:** Big uncertain bets, a founder frozen by the size of a plan, or a team that confuses courage with recklessness.
- **How to score:** No named payoff, no bet. Payoff named and risks managed: go. Then judge progress turn by turn, not by the distance to the bottom.
- **Quote:** "You take risks for reward" (S031)
- **Also:** "No risk is worth taking if there's no payoff" (S056) and "And if those risks can be managed, keep going, even in the face of uncertainty." (S038)
- **Sources:** 7 (S008, S031, S036, S038, S046, S056, S060)

## Time the money

### 6. Invest ahead of revenue, then someone has to pay
- **What it is:** Some businesses need usage before they can earn, because usage is what makes the product better. Google could not have covered search costs early and would never have gathered enough user feedback to improve results. But the bill comes: over time revenue must cover costs, and there are only a few ways to get there (S058, S059).
- **Parts:** (1) Does more usage make the product better (a learning loop)? If not, there is little reason to run ahead of revenue. (2) What will pay: advertising, businesses (data services, subscriptions), or users, likely a mix. (3) When does revenue have to cover costs? (4) Early Google example of a bold bet: a revenue guarantee to AOL big enough that Eric Schmidt checked on advertisers "every couple hours" until revenue covered it (S053).
- **When to use:** Free tiers, community before monetization, AI or software products with heavy usage costs, "growth now, money later" plans.
- **How to score:** Pass only if (a) usage measurably improves the product or the moat, (b) the payer is named, and (c) there is a date or milestone when revenue covers costs. Missing any one, treat it as hope, not strategy.
- **Quote:** "If we had waited to have a business model that could cover those costs Google wouldn't have won" (S059)
- **Also:** "People make it so complicated it's not complicated ready someone has to pay" (S058)
- **Sources:** 4 (S004, S053, S058, S059)

### 7. Long run over short run, said out loud
- **What it is:** Take the short-run revenue hit to secure the long-run position, and say plainly that you are doing it. Her examples: the 2012 mobile-first rebuild, when Facebook "disappointed a lot of people" on near-term revenue right after its IPO (S035); safety and privacy spending that lowered growth in 2019 (S043); paying every employee and contractor through COVID while ad spend collapsed (S044, S045).
- **Parts:** Name the trade (current revenue for future revenue). Size the hit if you can. Name who has to accept it (investors, the board, the team). Protect the decision with a structure that allows a long view; she credits Zuckerberg's control of the company for this (S035).
- **When to use:** Platform shifts, quality or trust investments, keeping people through a downturn.
- **How to score:** Is the future revenue tied to a real shift (customers moving to mobile, trust as a precondition)? Can you survive the hit? If yes to both, take it. If you cannot survive the hit, this framework does not apply.
- **Quote:** "We were giving up a lot of current revenue for the promise of future revenue" (S035)
- **Also:** "These things lower the revenue growth and they cost a lot of money but they're the right thing to do" (S043)
- **Sources:** 5 (S024, S035, S043, S044, S045)

## Scale the operation

### 8. Fly the plane versus catering and baggage
- **What it is:** Her core scaling framework (S056). An airline CEO told her airlines lose bags because they allow zero margin for error on maintenance and pilots, so all the mistakes land in catering and baggage. Managers wrongly pick one style for everything: tight operator or move fast. You need both, and the skill is sorting.
- **Parts:** (1) List the work. (2) Mark each item: plane (a failure is catastrophic or trust-breaking, such as data security, payments, safety) or baggage (a failure is fixable tomorrow, such as a feature set, a launch detail). (3) Run planes with checks and no shortcuts. (4) Ship baggage imperfect and iterate; at Meta, Reels and Threads launched with imperfect feature sets (S056). (5) A small company can run everything loose; as it grows, it must sort.
- **When to use:** Any scale-up plan, any argument between "move fast" and "be careful," launch readiness reviews.
- **How to score:** For each risk in the decision, is it plane or baggage? A plan that treats a plane item casually fails. A plan that treats baggage like a plane ("check it 8,000 times") never ships and gets passed.
- **Quote:** "The most important thing to learning how to scale is you know a little company you can run anything you want but as you get bigger you got to figure out like what is maintenance and what is baggage" (S056)
- **Also:** "Data security yeah that's operating the plane like we couldn't have data breaches" (S056)
- **Sources:** 2 (S056, plus the speed-versus-control lessons in S012 and S053)

### 9. Build now for the size you will be
- **What it is:** Promises and perks that work at 4 people break at 400, and taking them away hurts more than never giving them. At Google she promised her four-person team that everyone would interview everyone, and withdrew it two weeks later at 12 people; birthdays went from the day, to the week, to quarterly sheet cakes for 4,000 (S035).
- **Parts:** For every process, perk, or promise, ask what it looks like at 10x. Design that version now. A big company is "a big dog" that has to be trained, with systems in place; small companies get away with chaos (S056).
- **When to use:** Early hires, culture rituals, approval processes, founder-only steps.
- **How to score:** Does the plan include any promise or process that cannot survive 10x headcount or 10x customers? Each one is a future betrayal. Fix it before launch.
- **Quote:** "I'm not saying be mean and don't celebrate birthdays I'm saying figure out what your systems are gonna look like later and do it now" (S035)
- **Sources:** 3 (S009, S035, S056)

### 10. Small teams inside a big company
- **What it is:** Innovation survives scale when work is cut into small teams. Her proof: Facebook Lite, rebuilt from scratch by four or five people (S003); teams of about six inside a company of thousands (S009); every engineer shipping code in week one (S009).
- **Parts:** Break big tasks into small teams with a clear owner. Keep the best ideas coming from the bottom. Remove status markers (no executive dining room, no special parking; she and Zuckerberg sat in the middle of the open floor) (S012).
- **When to use:** A growing team that has slowed down; a new product inside an established company.
- **How to score:** Who owns this, and how many people are on it? A big committee with shared ownership scores low.
- **Quote:** "If you're working with a team of six people, you can be doing that in a company of thousands." (S009)
- **Also:** "Make sure you run a system where the best ideas come from the bottom, not the top." (S012)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S009, S012)

### 11. Hire for skills, ahead of need, and different from you
- **What it is:** Her hiring operating system, built while her Google team grew from 4 to about 4,000 (S035, S056).
- **Parts:** (1) Skills over experience, because in a new industry "almost none of us have done anything we do now before" (S017); she did not look for online ad sales experience because no one had it (S035). (2) Temp-to-hire to scale fast and find people who "didn't necessarily interview well" (S035). (3) Hire ahead of when you need them (S056). (4) Fire sooner; "we usually fire people too late" (S056). (5) Hire people better than you and different from you, including in personality and thinking style (S035). (6) Hire people who spot problems and bring solutions (S030).
- **When to use:** Any hiring plan, especially for roles that did not exist before.
- **How to score:** Is the hire scored on demonstrated skill or on a résumé match? Is it timed before the crunch or after? Is there a known underperformer being kept too long?
- **Quote:** "If you hire only based on experience as Industries change experience is less relevant but if you hire based on skills you can then adapt" (S017)
- **Also:** "Try to hire people ahead of when you need them" (S056) and "We usually fire people too late" (S056)
- **Sources:** 6 (S017, S030, S031, S035, S056, S013)

### 12. Define the culture and repeat the mission
- **What it is:** An undefined value becomes whatever each person projects onto it. Google interviewed for being "googly" without defining it, and a later study found introverts looked for quiet people and extroverts for social ones (S056). People follow a leader willingly only when the mission is aligned, so you repeat it constantly (S035).
- **Parts:** Write down what each value means in behavior. Repeat the mission "over and over and over," even when everyone knows it. Tell people the destination, not the steps.
- **When to use:** Hiring criteria, scaling a team past the point where the founder knows everyone, any culture word on a wall.
- **How to score:** Can two interviewers score the same candidate on a value and agree why? If not, the value is undefined.
- **Quote:** "You really have to define what you mean" (S056)
- **Also:** "You have to repeat your mission and your purpose and the values you care about over and over and over" (S035)
- **Sources:** 4 (S010, S035, S056, S007)

### 13. Optimize the whole system, not the subsystem
- **What it is:** Give people a clear metric they control and they will run at it while missing everything else. Her soccer parable: bonus offense on goals scored and defense on goals allowed, and you get an offense that would rather lose 10 to 15 than win 3 to 2 (S012, told again in S056).
- **Parts:** Pair individual metrics with a shared outcome. Use equity so "everyone rises or falls" (S056). Check every target for how it could be gamed against the whole.
- **When to use:** Comp plans, sales commissions, team OKRs, partner revenue shares.
- **How to score:** For each metric in the plan, write the cheapest way to hit it that hurts the company. If that path is easy, add a whole-system measure.
- **Quote:** "If you optimize the subsystem you often sub optimize the big system" (S056)
- **Also:** "I now have an offense which would rather lose a game 10-15 than win a game 3-2." (S012)
- **Sources:** 2 (S012, S056)

### 14. Platform and partners over doing everything
- **What it is:** Build the core technology and let others build on it, so you can "win along with lots of other people winning" (S011). Facebook did not build every app; it built the social graph and feeds, with a million developers on top (S004). With acquired companies, she kept "product control" with the founders who ran them (S024).
- **Parts:** Name your core. Open the rest. Do not compete with your partners' products. When you buy a company, keep the founders in charge of product and plug them into your distribution (the Instagram ads product, built by Instagram and sold through Facebook's sales force, S024).
- **When to use:** Build versus partner decisions, acquisitions, a founder tempted to own every step of the customer journey.
- **How to score:** Does doing this yourself strengthen the core, or spread the team thin? Would a partner do it better and send value back?
- **Quote:** "We don't try to build every single thing our users will want to use, but we focus on building the core technology." (S004)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S004, S011, S024)

## Lead the people

### 15. Process agreement before substance
- **What it is:** Before joining a founder or forming a partnership, do not try to settle today's disagreements; they will change. Settle how you will disagree. Dave Goldberg's advice to her before Facebook (S031, S035).
- **Parts:** She asked Zuckerberg only for a process: a one-on-one meeting first and last every week, and feedback to each other every week. He made it mutual. They agreed on it before she joined, and by 2017 she said it had run for nine years (S003, S031, S035).
- **When to use:** Co-founders, a key executive hire, a joint venture, any partnership where the two people differ in age, style, or risk appetite. She describes herself as "more risk averse than Mark" and calls the difference a balance (S003).
- **How to score:** Is there an agreed cadence for disagreement and feedback? If the partnership rests only on agreeing about the plan, it scores low.
- **Quote:** "What you need to sort out before you take the job is the process. Can you and Mark disagree on something and work it out?" (S031), relaying Dave's advice.
- **Also:** "On the way in I didn't ask him for anything except a commitment to the process of our relationship" (S031)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S031, S035)

### 16. Make it safe to tell the leader the truth
- **What it is:** Hierarchy distorts information. Speaking truth as a leader is easy; hearing it is hard (S007). Her two signature failures prove it: at Google she was a hiring bottleneck and her reports applauded when she offered to stop interviewing; at Facebook her "no PowerPoint in my meetings" rule became "no PowerPoint with clients" (S013, S035, S056).
- **Parts:** (1) Name your own weaknesses in public ("I often speak too much"), which gives people permission to agree (S010, S013). (2) Reward honesty visibly: she and Zuckerberg tracked down an intern who told Zuckerberg to improve his public speaking and hired him (S010). (3) When nobody told you, treat it as your failure. (4) Tell people to fight or ignore stupid rules, even yours (S013).
- **When to use:** Any decision where the founder's view dominates, and any time a team has gone quiet.
- **How to score:** When did someone last tell the founder they were wrong, and what happened to them? No recent example is a warning.
- **Quote:** "What's hard when you're a leader is hearing the truth." (S007)
- **Also:** "I thought to myself I've become a bottleneck and you didn't tell me and that's on me" (S035)
- **Sources:** 7 (S007, S009, S010, S013, S035, S053, S056)

### 17. Debrief everything, including the wins
- **What it is:** Learned from the Marine Corps at Quantico. If you only debrief failures, debriefs become finger-pointing. Make it routine, a few minutes at the end of every meeting, and debrief successes too, so you learn why things worked (S056, S060). Organizations that hide failure "don't learn" (S035).
- **Parts:** Short, routine, both directions. Ask what went right as long as what went wrong. Share lessons openly, so it becomes safe to say "I really got that wrong" (S027).
- **When to use:** After launches, quarters, campaigns, and failed experiments.
- **How to score:** Is there a scheduled debrief? Does it cover wins? Is failure shared or buried?
- **Quote:** "The way you build a resilient organization is you learn from failure you don't hide it you embrace it" (S035)
- **Also:** "You owe it to yourself to spend as much time thinking about what went right as what went wrong" (S060)
- **Sources:** 5 (S012, S027, S035, S056, S060)

### 18. Persuade with the team, do not decree
- **What it is:** Lead by listening and by deciding with the people who own the work. In 2008 she and Zuckerberg put a "Facebook congratulates Michael Phelps" banner on US pages over a weekend, against the no-editorial-voice policy and without the homepage teams. The company "went crazy." They apologized and committed not to decide again without the owning teams (S010). When she has to say "I've heard your objections, here is what we're doing," she counts it as a failure.
- **Parts:** Get ideas from the team. Explain. Decide with the owners. Keep top-down calls for real emergencies; she concedes militaries, and sometimes companies, need them (S053).
- **When to use:** Any decision the founder wants to make alone over a weekend.
- **How to score:** Were the people who will execute it in the room? If not, expect the plan to stall or backfire.
- **Quote:** "We're not going to make decisions again without the teams who work on those products." (S010)
- **Also:** "Mark and I don't run Facebook. You know, we lead Facebook." (S003)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S010, S013, S053)

### 19. Full accountability, then prove it with spending
- **What it is:** Two linked moves. Personally: choose the explanation that keeps control with you. Her commute story: the same 20-minute drive for 14 years, then a crane makes it 40; was she late because of traffic or because she did not leave early enough? Choose the second and you take back control (S060). Organizationally: after Facebook's 2016 to 2018 failures, she said apology is not enough, and pointed to where the money went (S040, S041).
- **Parts:** Own it fast and specifically ("that's on us"). Then act, and show the commitment in budget and headcount. Safety and security headcount went from about 10,000 to 20,000 in 2018 and later about 30,000 (S037, S041).
- **When to use:** After a miss with customers, a public mistake, or a broken promise.
- **How to score:** Is there a specific admission, a specific fix, and money or people behind it? Missing the spending, it is PR.
- **Quote:** "If you want to look at what companies care about you look where they spend their money" (S041)
- **Also:** "It's not enough to be sorry what matters is the action we are taking" (S040)
- **Sources:** 7 (S037, S038, S040, S041, S042, S043, S060)

### 20. Bias interrupters and criteria set in advance
- **What it is:** Her decision-quality tool for hiring, promotion, and reviews. First surface the bias with data, then build a mechanism that counteracts it. Organizations that believe they are meritocratic "actually have the most bias" (S023).
- **Parts:** (1) Ask "would we say this about a man?" when a review calls a woman "too aggressive" (S014, S018). (2) Fix self-nomination gaps, as Google did by telling managers the pattern and encouraging women to nominate (S023). (3) Standardize interview questions and set review criteria in advance, applied to everyone (S058, S059). (4) Make access equal: group lunches for everyone, rather than no one-on-ones with women (S042).
- **When to use:** Hiring rounds, promotions, performance reviews, choosing who gets the stretch assignment.
- **How to score:** Were criteria written before candidates were seen? Is the same question asked of everyone? Does feedback describe work, or style?
- **Quote:** "You establish criteria in advance that everyone agrees to that are universally applied" (S058)
- **Also:** "So you understand the bias, and you create the interrupter." (S023)
- **Sources:** 9 (S014, S015, S018, S023, S034, S042, S050, S058, S059)

## Stay close to customers

### 21. Listen to customers to set the roadmap
- **What it is:** Ask customers what they need, in order, and ship that. During COVID, small businesses told Facebook first they needed cash, then gift cards and fundraisers, then training; the roadmap followed (S045, S050). Tools alone fail: "building tools isn't good enough unless people can use them" (S050).
- **Parts:** Hold roundtables and ask for what is not working, not only what is. Sequence the asks. Pair every tool with training. Do not restrict how customers use help; they "know better what they need" (S045).
- **When to use:** Product roadmaps, launch plans for non-technical customers, any time a team assumes it knows the customer's priority.
- **How to score:** Can the team name the customer's top three needs in order, with sources? Is there a training or adoption plan, or only a feature?
- **Quote:** "I want to hear what's going well but I really want to hear uh the tools you need" (S050)
- **Also:** "We see small businesses know better what they need" (S045)
- **Sources:** 6 (S045, S047, S049, S050, S051, S052)

### 22. Iterate in public, do not over-plan
- **What it is:** Ship small and often, then learn. In 2009 a marketing chief told her it took 12 days to approve a post; she called it "a really serious problem" and told companies to spend half an hour a day instead of a month building the perfect page (S003). The same advice to small businesses a decade later: keep trying new things and see what works (S052).
- **Parts:** "Done is better than perfect" (S015). Post often, watch response, adjust. Hire the people who will move fast rather than a committee that is "too strategic" (S012).
- **When to use:** Marketing, content, early product, any team stuck in approval loops. Not for plane-level risks (framework #8).
- **How to score:** How long from idea to customer feedback? Days is good; months is a red flag for anything that is baggage.
- **Quote:** "I think the biggest mistake that uh businesses make is uh they do it, they plan too much. They want the perfect page." (S003)
- **Also:** "The biggest piece of advice we have is do it quickly and iterate." (S004)
- **Sources:** 7 (S003, S004, S011, S012, S015, S049, S052)

### 23. Prove it rings the cash register, with someone else grading
- **What it is:** Marketing and product claims must tie to sales, and the proof is stronger when a third party measures it. She built Facebook's ad pitch on Nielsen brand-lift studies and sales cases (S003, S006). When Facebook found an error in a video metric in 2016, she leaned on outside measurement: "We don't just grade our own homework" (S026).
- **Parts:** Define the business outcome (sales, not recall). Pick a measure an outsider can verify. Show a named customer with a number (Einstein Bros Bagels had its best sales week of the year after a Facebook campaign, S006; Gatorade lifted household sales 5%, S032).
- **When to use:** Any decision justified by "brand," "awareness," or "engagement."
- **How to score:** Is there a sales-linked metric, and who checks it? Self-reported vanity numbers score low.
- **Quote:** "People-based marketing can't just help people remember ads, it has to drive to sales because that's what marketers need at the end of the day." (S006)
- **Also:** "What matters for marketers is when they spend on marketing, it rings the cash register both online and off" (S032)
- **Sources:** 5 (S003, S006, S026, S032, S052)

## Recover from the hit

### 24. The three Ps *(Option B, 2017)*
- **What it is:** From Martin Seligman's research, which she learned after Dave Goldberg died in 2015. Three beliefs slow recovery from a setback: personalization (it is all my fault), pervasiveness (it affects everything), permanence (it will last forever) (S025, S030).
- **Parts:** Personalization: not personalizing is different from refusing responsibility, which "you should always do" (S025). Pervasiveness: her first job at the World Bank, when her boss found she could not use Lotus 1-2-3 in front of everyone; she was not bad at everything, only at spreadsheets (S025, S030). Permanence: we project current feelings forward indefinitely.
- **When to use:** A founder after a failed launch, a lost client, a bad quarter, or a team that has stopped taking risks after a miss.
- **How to score:** Listen for "always," "never," "everything," and "my fault" in how the setback is described. Each one is a P to correct before deciding the next move.
- **Quote:** "Not taking failures personally, allows us to recover, and even to thrive." (S025)
- **Also:** "You are not born with a fixed amount of resilience. It's a muscle. You can build it up and then draw on it when you need it." (S025)
- **Sources:** 9 (S025, S028, S029, S030, S033, S044, S046, S056, S060)

### 25. Returning to work: time off, plus specific praise *(Option B)*
- **What it is:** A management corollary she learned as the recipient. Her old reflex with a struggling colleague was to take pressure off, which she later realized can signal "you can't do your job." When she fell asleep in a meeting after returning, Zuckerberg told her a specific good point she had made that day (S029, S030, S055).
- **Parts:** Offer the time off and policies first. If they choose to be at work, find real contributions and name them. Ask what they want, rather than assuming (her "Platinum Rule," S055).
- **When to use:** A key person going through a hard time, a founder deciding whether to pull someone off a project.
- **Quote:** "What I needed to build up my confidence wasn't just permission to be a mess but also reassurance that sometimes I wasn't" (S029)
- **Sources:** 6 (S029, S030, S033, S034, S055, S056)
