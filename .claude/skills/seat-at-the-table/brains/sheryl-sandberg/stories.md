# Stories: Sheryl Sandberg

18 stories and case studies she reuses, most-told first. Figures are as she tells them; some drift between tellings (for example "12 times" versus "13 times," 60% versus "more than 50%"), and those are noted. Personal stories are included only as she tells them in public.

1. **The spreadsheet and the rocket ship (2001).** After Treasury, in the dot-com bust, she job-hunted for months; one CEO told her "we would never even think about hiring someone like you." She built a spreadsheet of jobs against criteria. The Google job, "business unit general manager" with no business units, met none of them, and several offers were more senior. Eric Schmidt put his hand on the sheet and told her not to be an idiot: take the rocket ship. Lesson: growth and mission beat title, and you cannot plan a career in a changing industry. (S013, S017, S031, S035, S053, S056)

2. **The tractor job.** At Google, with no CFO, she first did investor work for about three months. Then she asked Omid Kordestani to let her run the four-person self-serve AdWords team. He called it a tractor job for a Porsche; she said she was happy to be a tractor because she believed it was the future of the business. Her team grew from 4 to about 4,000 in six years. Lesson: take the unglamorous job at the core of what will grow. (S035, S053, S056)

3. **The interview bottleneck.** She promised her early Google team everyone would interview everyone, withdrew it at 12 people, and still personally interviewed every hire. At about 100 people she offered to stop, and her direct reports applauded; one called her "the bottleneck of all time." She was embarrassed, then angry, then owned it: they had not told her, and that was her fault. Lesson: you overestimate your own importance, and you have to make it safe for people to say so. (S013, S035, S056)

4. **No PowerPoint.** She banned PowerPoint in her own meetings to get more discussion. People ignored it for about two years; when she made it a rule, the organization turned it into "no PowerPoint with clients," which clients disliked. She apologized on stage at the global sales conference: "it is on me." Lesson: at scale, a leader's words get amplified, so tell people to fight or ignore stupid rules, even yours. (S013, S035)

5. **Dinners with Mark and the process agreement (2007 to 2008).** She met Zuckerberg at a Christmas party in December 2007 and they talked about scaling companies. Then came dinners at her house once or twice a week, from 8 until she kicked him out near midnight, which she treated as mutual interviewing. Facebook had about 70 million users, slowing growth, and the Beacon controversy; she turned down CEO offers to be "the second person." Dave Goldberg told her to agree not on substance but on process. Lesson: settle how you will disagree before you join. (S003, S031, S035, S053)

6. **The $1M mistake and Larry Page.** At Google she made a mistake that cost about a million dollars. Larry Page told her not to repeat it, but that he would rather see mistakes from moving too fast than from being too conservative. Lesson: bias toward speed; the mistake of doing too little is worse. (S012, S053)

7. **The AOL guarantee.** Google paid AOL a large revenue guarantee to carry its search results and ads against Overture. Eric Schmidt stopped by her cube every couple of hours asking about advertisers and revenue until revenue covered the guarantee, and then "we all knew this is gonna work." Lesson: the founders were bold, and a big bet can be right if you watch it closely. (S053)

8. **The airline CEO and the baggage.** On a bus to a Microsoft CEO summit she asked an airline CEO why airlines lose bags. His answer: zero margin for error on maintenance and pilots means every mistake lands in catering and baggage. She turned it into her scaling framework: sort plane from baggage. At Meta, data security was the plane; Reels and Threads could launch imperfect. Lesson: no single management style works for everything. (S056)

9. **The soccer team.** Pay the whole team on wins and an MBA says only pay people for what they control: offense on goals, defense on goals allowed. Now the offense would rather lose 10 to 15 than win 3 to 2. First told about philanthropy metrics, later about incentive design and equity. Lesson: optimize the whole system. (S012, S056)

10. **The Michael Phelps banner (2008).** Facebook had a strict no-editorial-voice policy. Phelps kept thanking Facebook in interviews, so she and Zuckerberg put up a congratulations banner over a weekend, without the homepage teams. The company "went crazy." They apologized and promised not to decide without the owning teams again. Lesson: decide with the people who do the work. (S010)

11. **Mobile first, and the next day nothing changed (2012).** Facebook declared mobile first at an all-hands, and the next day desktop screenshots kept coming. Zuckerberg ruled no product meetings without mobile screenshots first. Newly public, the company "disappointed a lot of people" on near-term revenue. Lesson: a strategy is not real until the operating rules change, and founder control can buy the long view. (S035)

12. **Beacon versus News Feed.** Both drew user protests. Beacon, before she arrived, was wrong and should have been pulled faster. News Feed drew the largest protest ever, Zuckerberg held his ground, and people used it more. Lesson: tell the difference by behavior, not noise, and pull mistakes back fast. (S003, S009)

13. **"I was wrong" on the 50% metric.** In 2009 she told the communications team to stop citing that 50% of users returned daily, because later adopters always engage less. People ignored her, and the figure held from 70 million to 350 million users. Lesson: she can be wrong, and a culture where people act on conviction can beat a cautious leader. (S003, S005)

14. **Too slow (2016 to 2019).** After Cambridge Analytica and Russian interference, she said Facebook "did not do enough to protect people's data," was "too slow," and "never foresaw" the 2016 interference. She pointed to safety and security headcount growing from about 10,000 to 20,000 in 2018, then about 30,000, and to safety teams embedded in every product team. Lesson, in her words: believing in the good can blind you to the bad, and apology without spending is not enough. (S037, S038, S040, S041, S042, S043)

15. **Small business proof stories.** She carries named owners as evidence: Einstein Bros Bagels' best sales week of the year after one Facebook offer (S006); a Brazilian accessories founder with 79% of sales from Instagram ads she made on her phone (S032); Fairytale Pet Care turning ads off because demand outran capacity (S051); Lush Yummies, whose national orders were expensive to fill until local targeting put "lines around the block" (S049); Vietnamese Foodies going online first, then to three restaurants (S052). Lesson: proof is a named customer with a number. (S006, S032, S049, S051, S052)

16. **COVID: pay everyone, cancel the review cycle (2020).** Facebook sent everyone home, paid employees and contractors whether or not they could work, canceled the first-half performance cycle because otherwise "no one's going to believe us," and paid more than 100% bonuses, while launching $100 million in small-business grants with no restrictions on use. Lesson: in a crisis, show by policy that it is not business as usual, and trust customers to know what they need. (S044, S045, S048, S050)

17. **Back at work after Dave died (2015).** Ten days after her husband's sudden death she went back to Facebook. She fell asleep in a meeting and got basic facts wrong, and told Zuckerberg maybe it was too soon. He told her a specific good point she had made that day. She realized her own habit of taking pressure off grieving colleagues could crush their confidence. Lesson: offer time off, and if they are there, find real work to praise. (S029, S030, S033, S034, S055, S056)

18. **Lotus 1-2-3 and the three Ps.** At her first job, at the World Bank, her boss discovered in front of everyone that she could not use Lotus 1-2-3. She thought she was terrible at everything; she was only terrible at spreadsheets. She uses it to teach pervasiveness, and the commute-and-crane story to teach full accountability without self-blame. Lesson: name the setback's real size, and choose the explanation that gives you control. (S025, S030, S060)
