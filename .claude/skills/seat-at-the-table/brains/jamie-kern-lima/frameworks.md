# Frameworks: Jamie Kern Lima

20 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (customer and product, brand and authenticity, rejection and conviction, money and risk, team and scale, self-worth). "Sources" counts distinct sources in the corpus that use the framework or a clear synonym of it. Book tags show where the framework sits in her books (*Believe IT*, 2021, her story; *Worthy*, 2024, which she calls the playbook), as she teaches them in public; no book text is used beyond what she reads aloud on her own channel. Quotes come from auto captions, so they keep caption quirks.

## Customer and product

### 1. The one-customer test ("Does this serve her?")
- **What it is:** Every product, channel, and marketing decision is run past one specific, real customer placed at the center of the company's vision. If it does not make her life better or solve her problem, it does not ship, however hot the trend.
- **Parts:** (1) Name her concretely. Before her first QVC airing she pictured a single mom in Nebraska folding laundry, too busy to remember she mattered (S033, S013). (2) Put her photo in the middle of the mission, physically: every employee desk sign and screensaver showed four photos of what IT Cosmetics stood for with the customer in the center (S041, S016). (3) When retailers or the sales team push a trend, go back to the photo and ask: does this serve her, is her life better, does it fit the vision (S041). (4) "Usually the answer is no": then you do not launch the product, or you do not go into that retail partner.
- **When to use:** Line extensions, trend products, a retailer asking for an exclusive, a marketing campaign, any choice between a short-term sales pop and the core customer.
- **How to score:** Can the founder name the customer in one sentence? Would she see someone like herself in the marketing? Does the option make her life better, or only hit a sales goal? An option that scores well only on "a retailer asked for it" or "competitors are doing it" fails.
- **Quote:** "And we would go back to that photo. I'd be like, Okay, does this serve her?" (S041)
- **Sources:** 9 (S041, S016, S033, S013, S001, S035, S042, S047, S003)

### 2. Show her someone like her (proof over aspiration)
- **What it is:** Her answer to the industry rule that people only buy from "images of unattainable aspiration." A customer cannot believe a product works for her unless she sees it working on someone like her, so the demo and the models are the proof.
- **Parts:** Real models of every age, size, skin tone, and skin challenge; the founder taking her own makeup off on live TV to show her bare rosacea; a live, unedited demo (why QVC was the dream channel: "prove live the product works I don't need a fake ad," S009). "Accessible is aspirational" (S020).
- **When to use:** Any marketing that leans on polished, idealized imagery; any product where the buyer's real worry is "will it work on me?"
- **How to score:** Does the buyer see herself in the proof? Is the demo real and unretouched? Customers who never had the founder's problem still buy when they feel seen: about 95% of IT customers had no skin issues (S050, S015).
- **Quote:** "If i'm sitting at home watching how do i know something's gonna work for me if i don't see it on someone who looks like me." (S007)
- **Sources:** 20+ (S007, S015, S002, S003, S004, S018, S031, S036, S047, S020, S050 and most QVC tellings)

### 3. Problem to market ("if I have this problem...")
- **What it is:** Her product origin logic: a painful problem with no working solution, despite hundreds of companies, is a market signal. Then go beyond solving it to a deeper mission.
- **Parts:** (1) Spend your own money trying every option (she spent her paycheck on drugstore, department store, and pro makeup, S006). (2) Check the gap is real: she had never seen anyone with problem skin in an ad and later cites that 70% of women have hyperpigmentation (S008). (3) Iterate until it works for you: "hundreds of iterations" on the first concealer (S016, S025). (4) Then add the deeper why (framework 6). She later says you do NOT have to solve your own problem; you need a why personal enough to sustain you (S013, S027).
- **When to use:** Evaluating a new product idea, especially one born from the founder's frustration.
- **How to score:** Is the problem painful and common? Has the founder tried the alternatives? Does a version exist that works? Is there a why beyond "it solves my problem"?
- **Quote:** "If i have this problem there must be so many other people out there that can't find anything else that works for them." (S004)
- **Sources:** 12 (S004, S006, S008, S011, S009, S010, S013, S022, S023, S031, S047, S027)

### 4. Great product is not enough: awareness and readiness
- **What it is:** Her most-admitted early mistake. She believed a product that works "is just gonna sell." It sold almost nothing for three years because nobody knew it existed and gatekeepers would not stock it.
- **Parts:** (1) Awareness: "for three years the reason why is nobody knew it existed" (S031). (2) Distribution: QVC became their advertising substitute because they "couldn't afford advertising" (S023). (3) Readiness: an early yes from a giant account can kill you if the back end is not ready (framework 11). (4) Timing: her innovative contour palettes did not sell, then bigger brands copied them and won when the market was ready; "just being first isn't always isn't always optimal" (S020).
- **When to use:** A founder who is sure the product will "sell itself," a launch with no distribution plan, a pioneering product in a market that is not ready.
- **How to score:** Who will hear about this, and how, with no ad budget? Is the market ready now, or are you early?
- **Quote:** "You can have the best price in the world but if people don't know it exists you know it's it sucks." (S006)
- **Sources:** 10 (S031, S006, S004, S021, S035, S002, S012, S010, S020, S023)

### 5. Scrappy customer testing
- **What it is:** Before chemists and clinicals she tested in the living room on herself, her first employee, and her husband, and used friends and family as focus groups. Women posting their own before and after photos online were her earliest proof (S002).
- **Parts:** Multiple data points even when tiny ("we needed multiple data points," drawing eyebrows on Paulo for 12 hours, S041); read customer letters, not just numbers (S043); watch what real customers post.
- **When to use:** Early product decisions with no budget for research.
- **How to score:** How many real people outside the founder have used it, and what did they do afterward (post, reorder, write in)?
- **Quote:** "I would have to draw them on Paulo too because we needed multiple data points." (S041)
- **Sources:** 5 (S041, S024, S002, S043, S027)

## Brand and authenticity

### 6. The why beneath the why *(Believe IT; Worthy ch. 16, "Know Your Why, Then Fly Girl Fly")*
- **What it is:** A resilience and alignment tool. The first why ("help people like me," "pay for college," "make money") sounds good when you share it, people clap, and founders stop there. That why is "why so many entrepreneurs fail" (S013). Peel back the layers until you hit something personal enough to keep you going for years.
- **Parts:** (1) Write the why for each goal; most people keep a goal journal and never attach a why (S027). (2) Peel: hers went from "a product that works for me" to "help millions" to the painful core: beauty ads made her feel not enough as a girl, so the mission became shifting the definition of beauty (S033). (3) The deep why need not be about the company: breaking a cycle of poverty in your family counts (S001). (4) Put it in front of the team (framework 18).
- **When to use:** Before quitting a job to start; when tempted to quit; when a team is losing focus; when choosing between money and mission.
- **How to score:** Would the why survive three years without a paycheck? Is it bigger than the founder? Money alone fails the test: "you can go get another job and get money" (S008).
- **Quote:** "A lot of times we have a why that sounds really really good but it's actually not deep enough to make us go for it." (S047)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S013, S015, S011, S006, S033, S026, S027, S056, S042, S047, S009, S019, S031)

### 7. Same on air as in the green room
- **What it is:** Her single most-repeated business observation, from over 1,000 live QVC shows and thousands of founders passing through the green room. Most brands aired once or twice and were gone. The survivors were not the ones with the best product, the most marketing money, the biggest discount, or the most TV skill. They were the same person on air as off air.
- **Parts:** (1) Show up fully, not as "your representative" (S014). (2) Do not copy what is working for a competitor; that dilutes "your own secret sauce" (S013). (3) "You can't try to sell because then nothing sells" (S004). (4) In some tellings a second trait: being "humble enough to be a student" of the craft (S012).
- **When to use:** Founder-led selling, live video, social content, choosing a spokesperson, hiring people to sell for you.
- **How to score:** Would a customer meeting the founder off camera meet the same person? Is the pitch a copy of someone else's playbook?
- **Quote:** "It was not who had the best product it was not who is the most famous it was not who's giving the best discount or Deal of the Day." (S005)
- **Sources:** 16 (S001, S003, S005, S012, S004, S013, S014, S018, S020, S019, S023, S033, S038, S050, S025, S007)

### 8. "You're not crazy, you're just first" (novelty test) *(Worthy ch. 3)*
- **What it is:** If you do something authentically your way, it has by definition never been done, so experts, buyers, and investors, who need social proof, will doubt it. Their doubt is expected, not diagnostic.
- **Parts:** (1) Expect experts to say no: they "cannot imagine something being successful unless it's already been done before" (S011). (2) A crowded category does not matter: she launched "a concealer then a foundation that has been done a million times before" and won by doing it her way (S005). (3) "Get really good at cheering for yourself," because most people cheer only after you have made it (S007). (4) Weigh critics by whether they have built anything: most beauty buyers who said no "never actually built anything themselves" (S016).
- **When to use:** When a novel idea is rejected by gatekeepers; when someone says "it's already been done."
- **How to score:** Is the rejection about the idea being new, or about a real flaw? Have the critics built something themselves?
- **Quote:** "When you show up authentically as a person or as a brand it's by definition never been done before." (S007)
- **Sources:** 18 (S007, S003, S004, S011, S005, S013, S016, S024, S036, S021, S044, S049, S031, S035, S027, S043, S052, S029)

### 9. Different is the enterprise value
- **What it is:** Staying odd is not only fulfilling; it is what makes a company worth buying. L'Oreal already owned about 40 brands doing the conventional thing; IT Cosmetics brought a new customer base and did not compete with the portfolio (S018).
- **Parts:** Resist packaging and positioning advice that would make you look like everyone else (L'Oreal itself told them to change packaging, S006); keep the price premium with real models, which retailers said was impossible (S010).
- **When to use:** Pressure to conform to get into a store, an acquirer's wish list, a rebrand toward category norms.
- **How to score:** If you made the change, would you look like an existing player in the acquirer's or retailer's portfolio?
- **Quote:** "We would have never created anything of value that was worth someone acquiring if we had changed everything we were doing to fit in." (S042)
- **Sources:** 8 (S018, S042, S008, S022, S014, S006, S021, S017)

## Rejection and conviction

### 10. No to future yes (the partner follow-up system)
- **What it is:** Her method for turning gatekeeper nos into deals over years. Professionally, never take a no personally; treat it as "this will be a yes one day, and then I'll decide if I want to partner with them" (S019).
- **Parts:** (1) Decode the no: a retailer no means "I don't think that we can make money from you" (S015). Ask why; "usually feedback is a gift" (S013). (2) Thank them in writing "as if I'm 100% certain we're going to be in their stores" (S025). (3) Keep sending press hits, sales updates, and launches for years (S003). (4) Go around the wall: LinkedIn messages and samples to anyone at the company, "product bombing" (S025); left her booth at a trade show to pitch a buyer; walked up to Ulta's CEO at a summit (S038). (5) Stay classy: the head of QVC beauty who said no later sat on her paid advisory board (S003).
- **When to use:** Any gatekeeper relationship: retailers, distributors, acquirers, investors, publishers.
- **How to score:** Does the founder still have a warm line into everyone who said no? Results she cites: Sephora said no for about six years, L'Oreal for three, and "every single no turned into a yes" (S025, S038).
- **Quote:** "I would still approach them as if it was going to be a yes and send them sales updates and send them product launches." (S003)
- **Sources:** 18 (S003, S025, S019, S023, S038, S004, S031, S002, S028, S042, S053, S015, S007, S018, S010, S012, S016, S017)

### 11. The readiness check (rejection as protection, in business terms)
- **What it is:** Her own revision of the "trust your gut" story. QVC's head of beauty was right to say no: had they said yes when she wanted, the company would have failed because it could not handle the back end of an account that size. Same for Ulta and Sephora, which needed a national education team (S026).
- **Parts:** Before chasing the big yes, check manufacturing capacity, compliance, working capital, and team. QVC's strict quality, regulatory, and clinical requirements later became a hidden blessing (S013). Even after QVC, "all of our money was tied up in working capital" (S021).
- **When to use:** A founder desperate for the big account, a big order from a giant retailer, a viral spike.
- **How to score:** If the yes came tomorrow, could you make, ship, fund, and support it? If not, the no may be protection.
- **Quote:** "Thank goodness we didn't get a yes sooner because we would have failed and we may not have gotten another chance." (S015)
- **Sources:** 9 (S015, S010, S019, S023, S038, S026, S027, S043, S021)

### 12. The Four Rs of rejection *(Worthy ch. 2, the longest chapter)*
- **What it is:** A four-step tool to change the meaning you attach to rejection, so fear of it stops keeping you stuck. She calls it "one of the keys on how I built the business" (S051).
- **Parts:** (1) **Reveal:** imagine a rejection and write your first thought; that is your current definition (hers: "yep, there's proof I'm not enough"). (2) **Redefine:** write a new definition that is empowering and that you truly believe ("rejection means I'm one of the brave ones"; "rejection is God's protection"; "I'm putting in the reps"), then intercept the old thought every time. (3) **Revisit:** go back to old rejections that took root in your identity and assign them new, true meanings. (4) **Revel:** enjoy being fearless about rejection and failure (S051, S057, S039).
- **When to use:** A founder avoiding pitches, sales calls, or launches out of fear; a team that will not float ideas.
- **How to score:** What does the founder do the day after a no? Hundreds of rejections are survivable only if each one does not mean "I'm not enough."
- **Quote:** "Had I actually assigned a lot of pain to every rejection I would have stopped and given up at like 20 rejections." (S040)
- **Sources:** 12 (S051, S057, S039, S040, S005, S021, S058, S059, S029, S046, S037, S036)

### 13. Gut check on expert advice (get still, then stet)
- **What it is:** How she decides when experts, buyers, mentors, or her team disagree with her. Listen to everything, then check it against your gut. Her biggest mistakes came when she followed an expert over her knowing; her defining moments came when she did not (S003, S014).
- **Parts:** (1) Get still: five minutes a day without the phone, often in her closet (S004, S056). (2) Ask "is that advice right for me?" (S043). (3) Accept the small, true catches (her font was too small) and write "stet" on edits that would change who you are (S014). (4) Remember advice comes "through the lens of their own experience" (S017). (5) Audit your history: list times you trusted your gut and times you did not, to build the muscle (S004). (6) When it is not clear, wait (S028).
- **When to use:** Any decision where the founder is about to override her own conviction to please an advisor, buyer, or investor.
- **How to score:** Is this advice about a fixable detail, or about changing the core of the product or the founder? Is the advisor seeing it through their own limits?
- **Quote:** "Sometimes experts give feedback and it's a gift and it's helpful. And a lot of times it's not." (S014)
- **Sources:** 20+ (S003, S014, S004, S043, S017, S015, S009, S032, S028, S035, S033, S027, S026, S031, S008, S056, S052, S050, S038, S025)

## Money and risk

### 14. Cash over flash
- **What it is:** Her first principle for new founders. Spend only on what adds value to the customer, the product, or the mission; never on what fuels ego or makes you look legit online. Stay cash flow positive above all.
- **Parts:** Economy rental car while other brands rented nice cars (S020); "Marie," her middle name, as the unpaid head of PR and customer service (S033); packaging designed on the living room floor by the TV station graphics guy at 4:30 a.m. (S006); not doing "anything glamorous or sexy or instagramable for many years" (S010); protect IP and formula ownership early (S014).
- **When to use:** Budget decisions, office and brand spend, agency hires, anything that "looks good online."
- **How to score:** Does this dollar serve the customer or the founder's image? Will it keep us cash flow positive?
- **Quote:** "I would protect every penny you can and invest it where it actually matters for the customer and where it matters for the quality of the product and the vision." (S035)
- **Sources:** 9 (S035, S033, S020, S010, S014, S016, S018, S006, S021)

### 15. The purchase order you can't afford to lose
- **What it is:** A rule she teaches and admits she broke once. Never accept an order or bet you cannot afford to lose; plan as if you could lose it and be okay, "otherwise don't accept it" (S006). She broke it on QVC's consignment order (over 6,000 units in 10 minutes, unsold stock returned unpaid) only because the alternative was going out of business anyway.
- **Parts:** (1) Can you survive a total loss? (2) If not, is the alternative already failure? (3) If you go, go all in on the authentic version: "I didn't have the luxury of trying it both ways" (S010). (4) Fund it the hard way: 22 banks said no, the 23rd gave an SBA loan just for the inventory, personally guaranteed (S011).
- **When to use:** Big consignment or exclusive deals, inventory bets, any one-shot opportunity.
- **How to score:** Downside survivable? If not, is this truly a last shot? She calls the rule "almost always true" (S011).
- **Quote:** "You should never say yes to this unless you're totally desperate and you're gonna maybe go out of business anyway." (S004)
- **Sources:** 20+ (S006, S004, S013, S011, S002, S003, S031, S053, S047, S038, S022, S025, S010 and most QVC tellings)

### 16. Don't sell the majority from desperation
- **What it is:** Her lesson from the private equity firm that passed: she was so desperate she would have given them most of the company for almost nothing. Because they and others said no, she and her husband were still the largest shareholders when L'Oreal paid $1.2 billion cash. She did later take private equity (TSG Consumer Partners) on good terms and calls it "an amazing experience" (S020).
- **Parts:** Raise from strength, not survival; a desperate partner may also change who you are and the vision ("they would've changed who I am or the vision of the company," S058); at exit, create competitive tension (framework 19).
- **When to use:** Early fundraising, especially when cash is nearly gone.
- **How to score:** Would this round give away control or force changes to the core? Is the founder raising because the plan needs it, or because she is scared?
- **Quote:** "We stayed the largest shareholder through the end through the sale partly because no one believed in us when we needed people." (S011)
- **Sources:** 18 (S002, S003, S004, S007, S011, S009, S020, S058, S028, S039, S040, S047, S031, S054, S059, S037, S017, S013)

## Team and scale

### 17. Replace yourself (key-person and key-channel test)
- **What it is:** Success hides a fatal flaw: a business that is the founder is not a business. IT was key-person dependent on her going on air 250 times a year and key-channel dependent on QVC, "a double double negative" for business health. Acquirers, L'Oreal included, saw it as too dependent on her (S016, S001).
- **Parts:** (1) Admit the founder signs off on everything (she approved every piece of copy, every story, every product name). (2) Diversify channels once the first one works: department stores, Ulta, Sephora, an infomercial that sold while she slept. (3) Replace yourself with authentic evangelists, not hired guns: three internal women whose lives the product had changed shadowed her for about three years, debuted in Canada where goals were gentler, and took over a $200 million QVC account (S016, S035). (4) Become "as irrelevant as possible" before an exit.
- **When to use:** Founder-led sales, a single dominant channel or customer, any exit plan.
- **How to score:** What breaks if the founder disappears for a month? What share of revenue comes from one channel?
- **Quote:** "When you can figure out how to replace yourself in every part of what you do the best you can that's the healthiest your business will be." (S016)
- **Sources:** 6 (S016, S001, S035, S010, S012, S014)

### 18. Mission as the operating system (hiring, retention, decisions)
- **What it is:** The deep why, made physical, becomes the filter for product, hiring, and marketing decisions across departments, and a recruiting edge when you cannot pay market rates.
- **Parts:** Desk frames and screensavers with the why, the customer at the center, and the four pillars of the mission; beliefs painted in conference rooms and bathrooms (S042, S041). Hire for "congruency of mission" (S016). Recruit people who "did not feel seen" at bigger competitors (S059). Boardrooms and leadership teams should look like the customer (S042). Repeat the competition rule until the team is sick of it: "our biggest threat to our success is not what our competition is doing, is if we ever get distracted by it" (S003).
- **When to use:** Scaling past the founder, cross-team disagreements, hiring against bigger competitors.
- **How to score:** Can a new hire state the why and the customer? Do decisions cite it?
- **Quote:** "It's easy as a leader to forget that your team may not truly know why they're doing what they're doing." (S035)
- **Sources:** 9 (S042, S041, S016, S035, S059, S003, S014, S044, S033)

### 19. Exit by competitive tension and partnership
- **What it is:** How the L'Oreal sale happened. Years of relationship (she sent L'Oreal's US luxury head a product basket after hearing her top-10 list), three years of meetings ending in no, then a market moment when another brand's sale left losing bidders hunting. They hired Goldman Sachs, ran a process, and said no to L'Oreal's first offer (S014, S001).
- **Parts:** Why sell rather than IPO: international expansion needed local regulatory and HR expertise in 100+ countries, which L'Oreal had; public-company CEOs warned "if you don't have to do it don't do it" (S010); and she did not trust herself to stop working 100-hour weeks if she kept it (S023).
- **When to use:** Weighing an acquisition, IPO, or strategic partner.
- **How to score:** Is there more than one bidder? Does the partner speed up the mission? What happens to the founder's life?
- **Quote:** "All of a sudden we had this competitive tension which drove the price way up." (S010)
- **Sources:** 9 (S014, S001, S010, S023, S047, S006, S026, S012, S042)

## Self-worth

### 20. Self-confidence vs self-worth, and the multiplier *(Worthy ch. 1 and ch. 14)*
- **What it is:** Her core Worthy distinction. Self-confidence is how you assess your skills against the outside world; it is fragile and fluctuates (the boxer who wins is about 30% more confident). Self-worth is "the deep internal knowing that you are worthy of love and belonging exactly as you are." Confidence, growth, and contribution are multiplied by self-worth to get fulfillment, and anything times zero is zero (S046, S005).
- **Parts:** Three ways low worth shows up in a business: (1) stuck, never launching or registering the domain; (2) going for it but hitting a ceiling (six figures, never seven) or sabotaging it; (3) achieving it and feeling empty (S044, S055). The thermostat: if you believe you are "worth 75," you will pull a business running at 90 back down (S051). Strong worth makes you more ambitious, not complacent.
- **When to use:** A founder stuck below a revenue ceiling for reasons that are not strategic; one who will not ask, pitch, raise prices, or share the big idea; a successful leader who will not take the next risk.
- **How to score:** Is the blocker skill (confidence) or a belief that she does not deserve it (worth)? Has she sabotaged wins before?
- **Quote:** "We'll want our business to be six figures or seven figures, we'll hit a ceiling at four figures, we don't know why." (S046)
- **Sources:** 20+ (S046, S005, S044, S055, S051, S045, S058, S057, S032, S037, S052, S059, S030, S028, S029, S019, S048, S049, S014, S017)
