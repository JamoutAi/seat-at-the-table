# Stories: Jamie Kern Lima

18 stories and case studies she reuses, most-told first. Figures are as she tells them; they sometimes vary between tellings, and the variations are noted.

1. **The one shot on QVC (September 2010).** A little over three years in, doing 2 to 3 website orders a day, she got one 10-minute live segment to about 100 million homes and had to sell over 6,000 units of Bye Bye Under Eye concealer, on consignment, or never come back. Paid consultants told her to use young, flawless models. She sat a week in an economy rental car outside QVC in West Chester, Pennsylvania, tempted to "do it their way first," then chose a bare-faced before shot of her own rosacea and models of every age, size, and skin tone. The host tossed her script two minutes before air and took over the wrist demo when her hand shook. It sold out at the 10-minute mark; her husband ran in saying "we're not going bankrupt," and she said "real women have spoken." One airing became 5 that year, 101 the next (150 or 151 in some tellings), then over 250 live shows a year for eight years; the largest beauty brand in QVC history, and in one account over $200 million a year. Lesson: stand for something, prove it live, and you cannot fake authenticity. (S001, S002, S003, S004, S006, S013, S047, S035, S025, S036, and about 30 more)

2. **"Someone who looks like you with your body and your weight."** About two years in, nearly broke, a large private equity firm that builds consumer brands into household names courted them through diligence, lawyers, budgets, and the product pipeline. In the final meeting the head investor, about three feet away, passed: he did not think women would buy makeup from someone who looks like her. She cried in her car, felt no anger, and decided "he's wrong." On the day L'Oreal announced the deal, six years later, he emailed "congratulations ... I was wrong"; it would have been his firm's most successful investment. Lesson: she would have given him most of the company for almost nothing; because he passed, she and her husband were still the largest shareholders. "This dude gave me a no but God gave me a knowing." (S052) (Tellings: S002, S003, S004, S007, S011, S047, S052, S022, S031, S059, and more)

3. **Alan Burke: "it's unanimous."** After she spent two and a half years "obsessively sending products," LinkedIn-messaging anyone at QVC, and begging his assistant for a call, the head of QVC beauty phoned to say the buying team had unanimously decided she was not the right fit. She cried three days. She kept sending him updates. Later he became a mentor, and when he retired IT Cosmetics hired him onto its paid advisory board: "the guy that rejected me is now working for me." Her later view: he was right, because they could not yet handle an account that size. Lesson: treat a no as a future yes, and check your readiness. (S015, S003, S026, S019, S023, S038, S053, S025, S035)

4. **Rosacea on air: the origin.** Anchoring weekend news in Portland, her rosacea broke through her makeup under hot HD lights "like desert clay" and the producer said in her earpiece "there's something on your face, wipe it off." She spent her paychecks on every makeup line and nothing worked. Insight one: if nothing works for her, many people have given up. Insight two: she had never seen a woman with rosacea in a makeup ad. Lesson: the problem plus the deeper why became the company. (S001, S006, S047, S031, S011, S022, S004)

5. **The honeymoon business plan.** She and her husband Paulo wrote the business plan on their 2007 honeymoon flight to South Africa ("the least romantic way to start any marriage"), came home, both quit their jobs, and put every penny of savings plus a little from friends and family into product, R&D, and an advisory board. It was over three years before they could pay themselves. Lesson: she did not know what she did not know, and that was partly a gift. (S001, S054, S047, S013, S006)

6. **HTML for Dummies and the first order.** No one would stock them, so they went direct to consumer; Paulo built the site from the big yellow "HTML for Dummies" book. Launch day: no orders, then days and weeks of none. She accused him of breaking it. The first order was Paulo, placed to prove the site worked. Lesson: a great product does not sell itself. (S001, S035, S010, S028, S006, S047)

7. **22 banks.** To fund the QVC inventory they applied for SBA loans; 22 banks said no, and she says they should have, because "we had no accounts receivable." With part of their last $1,000 they bought IKEA desks for the living room because the 23rd, California Bank and Trust, was doing an in-person office tour. It said yes, for just the purchase order and a little more, personally guaranteed; she spent the extra on the consultants whose advice she then ignored. Lesson: belief and scrappiness can substitute for collateral, once. (S008, S025, S011, S041, S007, S013)

8. **The Sephora elevator.** She could barely afford the flight to Sephora's San Francisco headquarters. The head buyer, eyeing her non-designer outfit, cut her off: if women were talking about the product, "I'd be hearing about it and I'm not." She held back tears in an elevator full of strangers ("I'm a CEO, I cannot cry") and then sobbed against a brick wall on Market Street. Sephora said no for about six years, even after IT was number one on QVC. Lesson: every no eventually became a yes; Ulta was the first big retailer to go all in and became their number one store. (S010, S019, S028, S025, S038, S053)

9. **Marie.** Unable to hire, she gave her middle name its own email address, and "Marie" became head of PR and head of customer service, pitching Good Morning America that "our founder Jamie Kern Lima is available." Mostly ignored, but "she got some press." Lesson: throw your ego out the window and be scrappy. (S033, S026, S014, S015, S054, S043)

10. **The knockoff.** After IT gained momentum on QVC, a giant competitor paid off IT's manufacturer to get the formula and launched an exact copy on QVC and in department stores. IT legally owned the formula but could not afford to sue. She felt sick for months and stayed professional in the shared green room. The copy "just did okay" while IT's same formula kept selling out. Lesson: a product that does not fit a brand's positioning fails even if the formula is great; customers are smart. (S021, S035)

11. **Replacing herself on a $200 million account.** QVC did not want anyone but her on air, so IT was dependent on one person and one channel. Instead of hiring a TV sales star, she picked three employees whose lives the product had changed, had them shadow her at every show for about three years, debuted them in Canada where the goals were gentler, and handed them the QVC business. Lesson: replace yourself before an exit; acquirers balk at key-person dependency. (S016, S035, S010, S001)

12. **The L'Oreal deal.** Years earlier she mailed L'Oreal's US luxury head a product basket after hearing her list her top 10 products, saying IT would be on it if she tried it. Three years of meetings, escalating to Paris, ended in no; L'Oreal also told her to change her packaging. When another brand's sale left losing bidders hunting, IT hired Goldman Sachs, ran a process, and said no to L'Oreal's first offer. L'Oreal came back with $1.2 billion cash for 100% in 2016, its largest US acquisition, and made her the first woman to hold a CEO title of a brand in its 100-plus-year history. She stayed three years on her word and says the business doubled in the first two years. Lesson: being different made them worth buying, and competitive tension set the price. (S014, S001, S010, S047, S018, S026)

13. **Why she sold instead of going public.** Going public might have made more money, and she fantasized about ringing the bell, but she and Paulo were addicted to work after about ten years of 100-hour weeks and a decade of fertility struggles, and international expansion needed local regulatory and HR expertise L'Oreal already had in 100+ countries. "I had to trust myself not to trust myself." Afterward she did grief counseling: it felt like raising a child and giving it to a stranger. Lesson: know your own limits, and partner for speed. (S042, S023, S012, S008, S046, S027)

14. **The single mom in Nebraska.** Her customer avatar. In the rental car she imagined a single mom folding laundry, too busy to remember she was beautiful and mattered, and decided she would rather that woman look up, see women like her called beautiful, and buy nothing, than sell a lot and stand for nothing. Later the whole company kept the customer's photo at the center of every desk. Lesson: decide for one real person. (S033, S013, S042, S041, S026)

15. **Walking up to Ulta's CEO.** At a roughly 1,000-person beauty summit, after two years of no from Ulta's buyers, she beelined from the back of the room to pitch Ulta's CEO directly, then cornered the Guthy-Renker founders at the buffet. IT became the number one brand in all of Ulta and, in her telling, Guthy-Renker's most successful makeup infomercial. Lesson: go around the gatekeeper with grace. (S038)

16. **The Forbes list.** After the sale Forbes put her on its richest self-made women list. She tried to get off it and for about three years had event bios changed from "richest" to "most successful." The reporter told her most women decline to comment while men complain their number is too low. She changed her mind: hiding success robs other women of an example. (S024, S034, S006, S002)

17. **Oprah's phone number.** After a three-hour lunch at Oprah's house, about a year after the sale, Oprah gave her a cell number. She did not call for four years, telling herself she needed the perfect thing to say. The real reason: she did not believe she was worthy of being her friend. When she finally called, it led to teaching a course with Oprah. Lesson: confidence (a billion-dollar exit) is not self-worth, and low worth makes you sabotage the biggest opportunities. (S005, S055, S032, S045, S048)

18. **Denny's pancakes.** Fired from Denny's in Pullman, Washington for talking too much to customers while the kitchen took an hour to get pancakes out, she learned that if operations are not right, nothing else works. She built IT's operational infrastructure first even though it was not her passion. (S015, S026, S035, S008)
