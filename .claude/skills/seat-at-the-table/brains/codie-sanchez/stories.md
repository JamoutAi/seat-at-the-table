# Stories: Codie Sanchez

20 stories and case studies she reuses, most-told first. Figures are as she tells them; several drift between tellings (the first laundromat's price and profit, portfolio size, loss amounts), and those ranges are noted.

1. **The border and "green."** As a young journalist covering trafficking and smuggling on the US-Mexico border (Juarez, with a Howard G. Buffett Foundation grant), she saw women with her own last name in desperate situations and concluded the main difference between her and them was money. A professor put it as the only language everyone speaks is "green." She left journalism for finance to learn that language. Lesson: money is power and options, and the people without it follow rules others write. "Those without money they are the ones that have to follow the rules and those with money they're the ones that get to write it." (S009) (S002, S004, S009, S013, S014, S034, S043, S045)

2. **Uncle Eb's plumbing company.** Her uncle built a Phoenix plumbing business doing several million in revenue and roughly $1M to $3M a year in profit (figures vary by telling). After a cancer scare he wound it down instead of selling, because he did not know selling was an option; a big competitor picked up the clients for nothing. She calls it like lighting your house on fire, and it seeded her whole thesis about boomer business transfers. Lesson: a profitable business is an asset that can be sold, and most owners never find a buyer. (S002, S003, S006, S009, S042, S048)

3. **The first laundromat.** While working 60 to 70 hour weeks in finance, she bought a laundromat for about $100K that produced about $67K a year (some tellings call the $67K revenue), with a friend who knew laundromats as operator; she was the finder, closer, and cash. She hid it for years because it seemed lame, later rolled several together and sold, and eventually sold all her laundromats. She now calls laundromats a "gateway drug" and a bad business for anyone good at business. Lesson: start with something so simple you can't mess it up, and let a small win teach you the game. (S005, S007, S008, S012, S019, S030, S040, S042, S048, S058)

4. **"Leave dead bodies behind" at Vanguard.** At Vanguard she pitched her own Latin America role. It was approved and then given to a colleague because peers said she would "leave dead bodies behind" her; she had skipped party planning to focus. The colleague later worked for her and became CEO of a business when she exited. Lesson: find a meritocracy, and a painful rejection can free you. (S014, S017, S023, S033)

5. **Early and late at Goldman.** A public school kid among Ivy Leaguers, she arrived before managing director Jim and left after him until he noticed and gave her opportunities; she got multiple offers without an MBA or a fancy school. Lesson: when you lack credentials, outwork on duration and send updates nobody asked for. (S017, S023, S061)

6. **"Get your own boat" and "we get rich quietly."** At her last firm she pushed the CEO to raise money and find deals online. He told her that if she wanted to row a different direction she needed a new boat, and on a beach walk said "we get rich quietly here." She left, later called her own reaction "total victim mindset," and says he did her a favor and wanted to be her first investor. Lesson: try to get your old boss to write your first check, and get rich with others, out loud. (S003, S006, S019, S029, S033, S034, S047)

7. **The $12 million fraud.** A cannabis company she and partners backed turned out to have fabricated its books, with insiders involved; they lost about $12M (not all hers) and recovered some. She says she didn't sleep for weeks and her first feeling was shame. New rules: no deals with people known under a year, tranched milestone-based investing, separate banks and accountants, daily account access. Lesson: "The biggest mistakes I've ever made are never how's but who's." (S034) (S034, S041, S052, S056, S058)

8. **The friend who stole.** A partner she considered a best friend took money from a business and was public about it; separately, a deal with a friend lost "several hundred thousand dollars" and the friendship. Her rule became "No New Friends." Lesson: she skips diligence steps for friends, so now someone else on her team runs them. "Now I have a new rule and the rule is No New Friends" (S017) (S015, S017, S025, S030, S036, S040)

9. **"Has small infected your thinking?"** Bill Perkins, after years of watching her buy small deals, asked whether small had infected her thinking. She found it rude, then agreed, and raised her ambition from laundromats to owning the whole small business acquisition ecosystem. Lesson: get a peer who tells you you're lifting too light. (S012, S017, S018, S023, S033, S034, S048)

10. **Strike Fire Productions.** She paid her podcast producer $3K to $5K a month. The owner loved production and hated sales. She valued the company at about 1x profit, put in about $10K for a large stake with an earn-in tied to growth, brought him clients, and was paid $5K to $15K a month for a couple of years before selling it back because it was too small for her. Lesson: your expenses are your deal flow, and expertise can buy equity. (S020, S026, S035, S049, S058)

11. **Argentina and the one-trick pony.** Selling offshore US dollar funds in Latin America, she watched a new government make her business illegal within a month. Regulation later killed a money market business, a Brazil venture, and her cannabis returns. Lesson: one rule can end you, so be hard to kill and diversify. (S025, S034, S047, S048)

12. **The divorce and the exit tax.** In her late twenties she ended her first marriage, set a calendar date to fix it or leave, lost access to bank accounts for a time, and paid a lot with no prenup. Her father called it the "exit tax to freedom." She now calls it her hardest decision and marriage to her husband Chris, a former Navy SEAL, the best one. Lesson: put a date on big decisions, and always sign a prenup. "he said there's always an exit tax to freedom." (S012) (S012, S023, S025, S047, S050)

13. **Threads Refined.** A fashion styling startup that cost her about $200K and couldn't be sold for $100K. Lesson: "I couldn't sell it because I didn't have a business I had a job it was a barely profitable job and nobody else wanted it" (S022) (S019, S022, S043)

14. **Outside the dealbox: ships and protein.** She invested in an autonomous ships company and an athlete protein product, both outside her competence, and "lost all my money" in both. Lesson: "Your biggest business buying problem is actually typically that you think you're smarter than you are." (S024) (S024, S026)

15. **Stripe and Substack.** After a few chargebacks, a payment processor froze one company's revenue for 90 days and she funded payroll personally; a newsletter platform cut off her list over selling a community off-platform. Lesson: multiple processors in every business and never rent your only channel. (S022)

16. **Spencer's trash route.** A software founder in Austin posted in a neighborhood group asking if people hated their trash company, pre-sold about 150 households at about $33 a month, and bought a used city truck. She uses it to teach pre-selling and de-risking (keep asking what the worst case is, then what you would do next). Lesson: sell first, build second. (S027, S028, S042, S053, S059)

17. **Branson's island and the hero complex.** She turned down Richard Branson's island invite because she told herself a roughly $5M business would fail without her; friends who went made deals. Lesson: being the hero is a trap, and a business centered on you is a job. (S062, S065)

18. **YouTube's real math.** She made $360,000 from YouTube AdSense in 2024 and says it cost about $1.2M to produce. She calls it the most honest account she has given. Lesson: content is a flywheel for owned businesses, not a business by itself. (S051, S059)

19. **Mr. Yokohama and Japan's lost businesses.** A 73-year-old logistics owner in northern Japan with a 30% margin and 17 employees gave his business away because nobody would take it; Japan projects hundreds of thousands of profitable closures. Lesson: profitable businesses die without successors, which is the opportunity and the mission. (S038, S054)

20. **Calling the landlords in COVID.** Across her companies she called landlords, vendors, and lenders to say what they could pay instead of what they owed. Lesson: "They want money and some money is better than no money." (S066) (S066, S036)
